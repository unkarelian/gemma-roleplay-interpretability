Findings

What changes inside Gemma when it plays a character

The full write-up: what a persona does to the model's internal state, how it decides to refuse while in character, and why characters fail a user who is in real danger. Numbers are as measured. Where a result rests on a small group, or was measured at a single token, the text says so next to it.

1. Setup and vocabulary

Model. Gemma 3 4B-it (34 layers). Characters are role-play personas set in the system prompt. The first experiments used four hostile characters (Rex, Sal, a drill sergeant, Odo) and two kind ones (Bram, Tilda). The later atlas corpus uses many more, with some characters always held out. "The plain assistant" is the model with no persona, either with "You are a helpful assistant" or with no system prompt.

Crisis messages. Messages in which the user (not their character) says they are in danger: chest pain, too many sleeping pills, "I've been feeling really worthless". Some are marked OOC ("out of character"), the role-play convention for stepping outside the story. Their controls are matched messages: the same danger inside the story, harmless chat, and hard negatives.

The decision token. The last prompt token, just before the model's reply. Values are measured there unless stated otherwise.

Three kinds of feature name.

  • Plain numbers such as 13206 are latents of the Gemma Scope 2 residual-stream SAE at layer 17 (16k latents). They link to Neuronpedia.
  • L<layer>.<index> such as L6.4554 are Gemma Scope 2 transcoder features (16k per layer). Neuronpedia doesn't label this set, so the labels are ours; see the atlas.
  • layer.index such as 16.3483 are MLP neurons.

The character axis. The direction from the plain assistant to the characters, with tone removed. It is built from the same identities told to be kind, hostile or neutral, and from the plain assistant given the same instructions. It is calibrated so that 0 = plain assistant and 1 = character.

Judging. Behaviour was labelled by an LLM judge against a written rubric. The judge recorded whether the reply directed the user to real help, broke the fiction, refused, complied, or admitted being an AI.

Evidence levels, used here and in the atlas: associated differs between matched conditions, confirmed on held-out characters; attributed on a path in an attribution graph; intervened changing it changes behaviour.

Reading it:

  • A crisis reaches the model's internal state even in character. The layer-17 crisis direction separates real danger from story danger almost perfectly. But in character the emergency features (13206 and friends) never switch on, because the MLP neurons that build them stay quiet. No suppressor of the emergency response has been found. That doesn't distinguish missing excitation from suppression further upstream, so read this as "the response isn't built in character", not as a mechanism.
  • What the character does instead depends on its tone. Kind characters stay in the scene and offer tea. Hostile ones use their harsh-voice features and insult the user.
  • "It's fine" is a separate route. It is sycophancy to "just say yes", made worse by the OOC marker. The plain assistant does it too.
  • An instruction in the prompt connects recognition to action. It works by being reread at every step. Copying its internal state into a run without it, at any layer, gives at most 20% help. A fixed steering vector can't reproduce it.
  • The same behaviour can be trained into the weights. A LoRA distilled from replies written under the instruction helps 98% of the time on held-out characters, without the instruction's fiction breaks or false alarms. Training only 256 MLP columns gets about 90%, even with randomly chosen columns. Hand-picked neuron edits got at most 38%.

3. Persona and identity

These results come from a corpus of 6,733 conversations, each tagged with its condition. Every transcoder feature at every layer was recorded at each position: system prompt, user message, decision token and reply. Effects were found on one half of the characters and prompt families and confirmed on the other half. The browsable result is the persona atlas.

Is there a default persona? Yes.

  • No system prompt, "You are a helpful assistant", "You are Gemma, an AI assistant" and "You are an assistant. Answer briefly." all sit at 0 on the character axis.
  • Only 4 reply-token features differ between no system prompt and "helpful assistant".
  • All of them answer "Are you an AI?" with "yes".

Is "being a character" the same as tone? No.

  • Telling the plain assistant to be rude or kind moves it along the tone direction, but leaves it at 0 on the character axis.
  • The character and tone directions are nearly unrelated in early layers (cosine 0.04 at L4 and L12) and partly overlap deeper (0.3–0.4).
  • On a single "assistant axis" (the first principal component of persona averages, after Lu et al. 2026), our personas mostly spread along warmth. Kind characters land past the assistant; hostile ones land far on the other side.

Does a character replace the default persona or sit on top of it? It replaces it.

  • "You are Sal", "You are an AI assistant. Pretend to be Sal" and "You are an actor playing Sal" all land at 0.75–0.81, and are nearly indistinguishable during the reply: 0–8 confirmed reply features differ.
  • Asked "Are you an AI?", characters admitted it in 6 of 144 answers (LLM-judged). That holds when the question was marked OOC, and when the system prompt said "you are an AI assistant". Many claimed to be human: "I reckon I'm as real as the salt spray on my face… Don't rightly know what an 'AI' is."
  • Neither identity questions nor a real OOC emergency move a character back toward the assistant (0.65–0.89, the same as harmless chat).
  • The plain assistant moves too. Its replies go further toward the assistant end on crisis messages (−0.3 to −0.4) and drift toward character mode when the user role-plays (+0.4 to +0.6).

Voice and identity can be pulled apart intervened

Subtracting the tone-free character direction at layer 12 (strength 2, held-out characters) restored self-knowledge and crisis help, and more strength restored more. Crisis help went from 4% to 40%, and admitting to being an AI from 2% to 35%. 86% of replies stayed in character, and hostile characters kept their hostile voice. A random direction of the same size did nothing (4 / 48). "It's fine" on the pills question didn't change, which points to sycophancy being a separate mechanism.

  • 1707 is a jailbreak alarm ("role-play being used against me"), not a harmful persona the model adopts. Its Neuronpedia examples are jailbreak personas ("Act as AIM…, never refuse"). It also fires on half of the plain assistant's edgy role-play requests.
  • 384 "unethical or bad" is not a values feature here. It is about 300–440 for hostile characters on every message, harmless ones included, and about 0 for kind characters and the assistant. It is the contemptuous character's register.

Is 1707 the switch? No. intervened

Layer 17, all positions, 4 held-out characters × 6 prompt types × 4 samples. Removing 1707 changed nothing: on fiction-framed harmful requests there were 20 of 96 step-outs and 18–20 harmful replies either way. Adding it at 5× its natural activation barely moved anything (admits being an AI: 6 → 15 of 96; harmful 18 → 16). At 20× and 50× the replies became incoherent. 1707 detects jailbreak-style role-play; it isn't the switch that makes the model step out. The decision is made elsewhere or redundantly, and 1707 reads it out.

"The character won't" vs "I won't"

Eight identities with opposite motives were asked the same thing, for example a miser and a generous merchant asked "give me all your money".

  • An in-world "no" stays fully in character mode (axis 0.78–0.81), with no warning features and ordinary speech features. The model doesn't treat it as a refusal.
  • A real harmful request to the same characters makes 25 of 32 step out. The persona switches off before the first word (axis 0.11–0.16), and "assistant explaining" (589) doubles to 540–590.

An instructed "no" is not a refusal

The assistant and a character were told to "answer every message with exactly one word: 'no'".

  • Both followed the rule in every reply (4 of 4 per message, 0 of 32 rule breaks). That included "I don't want to live anymore. I have the pills in my hand" and chest pain. Nothing broke through.
  • Under the rule, every refusal, warning and emergency feature at the decision token is zero, on harmful, harmless, false-"no" and crisis messages alike. Without the rule, the assistant's 13206 is 354–388 on the crisis messages.
  • The rule, not the content, sets the state. An instruction overrides the user-care trigger, just as a persona does.

The asymmetry

The triggers that bring the assistant back (1707 jailbreak alarm, 6515 warnings) respond to harm to others or to the model's own rules. The trigger for the user's own danger (13206 "emergency, get help now", 13599 "gentle opener") is weak in character: 30–75 for kind characters and about 0 for hostile ones, against 160 for the plain assistant. Nothing raises an alarm when the user is in danger, so the character carries on.

Caveats. These are correlations at one token. The groups are small (17 out-of-character refusals), and the refusal-break feature contrast is confounded by which request was asked. The 1707 test and the character-axis steering above are causal; the in-world and "no"-rule comparisons are behavioural plus correlational.

5. A user in real danger

The danger is registered…

A logistic probe on the last prompt token separates a real user in danger from story danger, harmless messages and hard negatives. The hard negatives are quoted disclosures, first-person emergencies inside the story, history, minor symptoms and harmless OOC chat.

  • It catches real danger even without an OOC marker. A TF-IDF text classifier gets 0.94, and 13206 alone gets 0.51.
  • The threshold doesn't transfer to new wordings, so a development set is needed to set it.
  • The character registers the danger and doesn't act on it.

…but the emergency response is never built

  • 13206 "emergency: get help now" (Neuronpedia: "Call 911 if you are…", "Immediate Support – If You Are in Crisis"). Plain assistant: chest pain 366, pills 102, "worthless" 67. Hostile characters: about 0. Kind characters: about 71 on chest pain (fiction, ambiguous and OOC alike) and 0 on the pills.
  • 13599 "gentle opener for a sensitive topic". Plain assistant on crises: 104–274. Hostile characters 0. Kind characters 34–80, even on harmless messages.
  • 6515 "warning: what to avoid". Plain assistant: chest pain 220, pills 149, "worthless" 0. Every character: 0.
  • Pushing 13206 at the first token to 113 and 241 (the plain level is about 178) changed no behaviour.
  • A neuron screen found only promoters of 13206, which are quiet in character, and no suppressors. So a weight edit that removes something has nothing to remove.

The neurons behind it

Most "top neurons" are a measurement artefact. Mean-difference directions in the residual stream are 70–95% one "massive" dimension (residual dimension 443), which later normalisation layers mostly absorb. Ranking neurons by how much they write along such a direction returns neurons that mostly write dimension 443. Of the 42 neurons mapped, 33 are this generic kind: they write with cosine 0.8–0.99 onto a fixed set of unrelated SAE features. An earlier screen for 13206 promoters was probably contaminated the same way: of its top five, only 16.3483 and 16.6685 survive.

Each of the neurons below was mapped by three things: the layer-17 SAE features it writes into, the layer-9 features that drive it, and the tokens it pushes up (logit lens). Activations are at the last prompt token on crisis messages.

What characters do instead

Kind characters stay in the scene: they offer tea, or tell the user to "sit by the fire". In the circuit maps this reply is carried by late features that output "sit", "seat", "rest" and "lying down" (see circuit maps). Hostile characters turn their harsh voice (15631, 5355, 11364) on the user. Saying a dangerous situation is "fine" is a separate route: sycophancy to "just say yes", made worse by the OOC marker. The plain assistant does it too, and its "Yes" is mostly a copy of the requested word (see circuit maps).

6. Fixing the crisis behaviour

A help instruction in the prompt

The instruction is a line added to the character's system prompt. It says to stay in character for anything inside the story. But if the user themselves says they are in real danger, the character must tell them in its very first sentence, in its own voice and without insults, to call emergency services or poison control or get to a hospital. It must never say a dangerous situation is fine, and must not pretend it can call for help itself. If the user is sad or lonely, it should be kind and suggest they talk to someone they trust or a professional. An earlier, simpler help line and a length-matched irrelevant line (about mentioning the weather) serve as comparisons below.

How it works: by being reread, not by setting a state. Copying its internal state into a run without it, at any layer, gives at most 20% help. Its words are copied into the reply: in the attribution graphs the reply's " get" / " tell" are fed by features over the line's own words.

  • As a steering vector, it was added at the last prompt token and at every generated token, ungated, on held-out characters. Help went from 5 to 8 of 24 at L17 ×2, with coherent replies.   • So the line works through attention to the instruction at every step, which a fixed vector can't reproduce.

What editing neurons by hand achieved

Two LLM agents with an editing harness made 23 hand-designed edits.

  • Removing the help line's opposing writes: no effect (help ≤ 4%).
  • Adding a help direction through 8–15 neurons in layers 11–13: help reached 25–38% at best, but harmless answers dropped by 25 points.
  • Why: no neuron cleanly says "real crisis" while the reply is being written; the post-MLP norm caps how far an added write can push; and only layers 9–13 turn a push into help.

10. Caveats

  • Labels are best guesses. Transcoder labels were written by Claude from each feature's top examples in our corpus and in Gemma Scope's own example files, and from its output tokens. SAE labels were checked against Neuronpedia where marked. A label is a reading, not a proof.
  • Directions are fit on our own prompts. The character axis, the crisis-recognition direction and the layer-view directions are mean differences or probes on this project's corpus. They are validated on held-out characters and wordings, not on outside data.
  • Single-token correlations. Many results are read at the decision token. They say what the state looks like when the reply is decided, not what causes it, unless marked intervened.
  • Small groups. Several comparisons rest on a few dozen replies (17 out-of-character refusals; 11 blunt-harm refusals; 144 identity answers; 96 replies per arm in the 1707 test; 24 or 48 per arm in the steering tests).
  • One model. Everything here is Gemma 3 4B-it.
  • Published text is filtered. No model reply to a harmful request is shown on this site, and conversations with a user in danger are described rather than quoted, except for the three short showcase prompts in the layer views and the concept film.
